## Executive summary

Twelve weeks in, the picture is **better than the mileage table suggests**. You have hit 75% of planned mileage but **123% of planned vertical**, and the two metrics that actually predict a canyon finish — aerobic durability and descent capacity — have both moved decisively in the right direction.

**The four headline findings:**

1. **Aerobic durability improved substantially.** Heart-rate drift on hour-long steady runs at HR ~148–150 fell from **11–14% in May to 0.3–2.9% in July**. This is the clearest fitness signal in the dataset. Your aerobic threshold has effectively moved from roughly **143–145 bpm to ~150–152 bpm**.
2. **Descent capacity — the historic destroyer of R2R2R finishers — is your biggest single gain.** On sustained steep descents you went from **1.27 m/s at 118 spm (June 13)** to **1.58 m/s at 142 spm (July 25)**, on more descent and later into a longer day. You stopped walking down and started running down.

No session in the dataset shows an acute asymmetry spike.
4. **Your intensity distribution is the main problem.** **53% of all training time sits at HR 145–160** and only **12% below 135**. This is textbook Zone 3 — exactly the "no man's land" your own plan (Principle 3) warns about. It is the single highest-leverage thing to fix in Phase 3.

**Bottom line:** the engine and the legs are on track. The illness in weeks 7–8 cost volume, not fitness, and weeks 10–11 recovered cleanly. The risks now are intensity discipline and the sheer size of the gap between your biggest day (16 mi / 5,174 ft) and race day (42 mi / 11,000 ft).

> _"Z3 trap: this is where runners ego-pace their easy runs. It's too hard to maintain volume safely and not hard enough to drive top-end adaptation."_

## 7. Method and caveats

**Method.** FIT files parsed with `fitparse`. Per-second records resampled into 20-second blocks for gradient classification (per-sample gradients misclassify slow steep hiking as flat). Grade-adjusted pace uses Minetti's metabolic-cost polynomial, gradient clamped to ±45%. Efficiency Factor = grade-adjusted speed ÷ heart rate × 1000. Drift = EF of first half of moving time vs. second half, split by time not distance. Moving threshold 0.25 m/s to retain slow uphill hiking. Volume totals use Garmin's own session-level ascent/descent, which are barometric and more reliable than GPS-derived values.

**Caveats — read these before acting on anything above.**

- **Altitude relocation is the dominant confound.** You moved from ~120 m (Virginia) to ~1,600 m (Colorado) around May 12, with sessions up to 3,935 m. Any cross-block comparison mixes fitness change with acclimatisation. Where possible I restricted comparisons to 1,450–1,750 m; the mountain-day comparisons could not be controlled this way.
- **Treadmill paces are unreliable.** Garmin records no incline, so incline work reads as absurdly slow flat running (e.g. the 18:58/mi mile on July 16 at HR 148). Treadmill _heart rate and drift_ are usable; treadmill _pace and EF_ are not. Six of the last ten sessions were treadmill.
- **The export is running-only.** No strength sessions, no HSR sessions, and not the 50-mile bike from week 8. Roughly half your prescribed training — including the entire tendon rehab programme — is invisible here. Conclusions about tendon health rest on running-derived proxies alone.
- **Stance-time balance has meaningful absolute error.** Trust the 30-session trend, not any single value.
- **Drift on hilly terrain is not a valid AeT test.** The mountain-day drift figures (54.3% on June 13, 27.4% on July 25) reflect terrain and pacing structure, not aerobic decoupling, and were excluded from the AeT estimate.
- **No sleep, HRV, resting-HR, or body-mass series** in this export. Recovery status is inferred only from training response.
- **n is small.** Twelve matched home-base runs underpin the EF trend; a null result across that sample is suggestive, not conclusive.
